A method you can trust

Training works best when your dog feels safe enough to try.

PawPath Home uses positive reinforcement and humane, minimally aversive teaching. We focus on what to do, how to make it easier, and when to get qualified help.

Read our training approach →
01

Reward what you want to see again

Food, play, praise, sniffing, or a chance to go outside can all reward a behavior. The best reward depends on the dog and the moment.

02

Make the next step easier

If your dog cannot succeed, change the setup instead of correcting the dog. Move closer, shorten the time, or quiet the room.

03

Protect trust while you teach

Modern training does not need fear, pain, intimidation, or dominance. Clear information and fair reinforcement are enough for most foundation skills.

Questions, answered

Before you pick up the treat pouch

Quick answers about age, session length, treats, and when to bring in professional support.

How old should my dog be to start?

You can begin gentle, reward-based foundation games with a healthy puppy as soon as your veterinarian says it is appropriate. Keep sessions extremely short, protect sleep, and adapt around vaccination and socialization guidance. Senior dogs can learn too; ask your veterinarian if pain or sensory changes may affect training.

How long should a home training session be?

Most small and medium dogs do well with 3 to 5 minutes, several times a day. Stop while your dog is still interested. A short session you repeat often is usually more useful than one long session.

What if my dog ignores me at home?

That is information, not defiance. Reduce the distraction, move closer, use a better reward, or make the behavior easier. If your dog repeatedly fails, change the setup before repeating the cue again.

Do I need a clicker?

No. A short marker word such as yes or nice works well. The marker simply identifies the exact moment your dog earned a reward.

Can I use treats forever?

You will not need the same amount forever, but reinforcement never has to disappear. Fade the treat from your hand, then reward after the behavior and vary food, play, praise, and access to the environment.

When should I get professional help?

Seek qualified help for bite injuries, repeated growling or snapping, severe fear, separation distress, sudden behavior changes, or if training is not improving quality of life. Start with your veterinarian to rule out pain or illness.
